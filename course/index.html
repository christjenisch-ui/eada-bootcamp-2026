Lesson 1

What is Exploratory Data Analysis?

Exploratory Data Analysis (EDA) is the first conversation you have with a dataset. Before you build a model or draw any conclusion, you look at the data: what's in it, what's broken, what's weird, and what seems related to what.

The term comes from statistician John Tukey (1977), who argued that you should explore data with an open mind before you confirm any hypothesis with formal tests.

In one sentence: EDA is using summary statistics and charts to understand a dataset's structure, quality, and patterns — so you can ask better questions and avoid building on bad data.

Why bother?

Catch problems early

Missing values, typos, duplicates, and impossible numbers break models silently.

Understand shape

Is a variable symmetric or skewed? Are there outliers? That changes which methods you can use.

Find signal

Which features move together with the thing you care about? That guides feature selection.

Form hypotheses

EDA generates questions; later analysis tests them.

The four big questions

  1. What do I have? Rows, columns, types, meaning.
  2. Can I trust it? Missing, duplicated, capped or impossible values.
  3. What does each variable look like? Centre, spread, shape, outliers.
  4. How do variables relate? Correlations, group differences, redundancy.

The rest of this course walks through each one using the housing.csv file in this project.

Reading describe()

How to read this table like an analyst:

  • Mean vs. median (50%) — if they're far apart, the variable is skewed. CRIM has mean 3.61 but median 0.26: a few tracts with very high crime drag the mean up.
  • Max vs. 75% — a huge jump hints at outliers. CRIM goes from 3.68 to 88.98.
  • Quartiles stuck at one value — ZN is 0 up to the median, so most tracts have no large-lot zoning. That's really a "mostly zero" variable.
  • Suspicious limits — MEDV maxes out at exactly 50.0, shared by 16 tracts. The original survey capped values at $50k, so the true prices are unknown (they're "censored").

Question the columns, not just the values. Column B is computed from the share of Black residents in each town. Using it to predict house prices bakes racial bias into a model. EDA is where you notice this and decide — together with domain experts — whether a feature should be used at all. (This is why scikit-learn removed this dataset in 2022.)

Lesson 4

One variable at a time

Univariate analysis looks at the distribution of a single column: where its centre is, how spread out it is, its shape, and whether there are outliers. The histogram is your main tool.

Distribution

Shapes to recognise

Symmetric

Mean ≈ median. Example: RM.

Right-skewed

Long tail to the right; mean > median. Example: CRIM, LSTAT.

Capped / censored

Spike at a limit. Example: MEDV at 50.

Mostly zero

Example: ZN. Often better treated as "zero vs. non-zero".

Outliers with the box plot

A box plot summarises the same distribution in five numbers. The box spans the middle 50% of data (Q1 to Q3); the line inside is the median. The 1.5 × IQR rule flags anything far outside the box as a potential outlier.

MEDV box plot

Hover the box or the red points

An outlier is a question, not a verdict. Ask: is it an error (typo, wrong unit)? A cap (like MEDV = 50)? Or a real, rare case (a very wealthy neighbourhood)? Only delete data you can show is wrong.

Relationships between variables

Bivariate analysis asks how two variables move together. For two numeric columns, use a scatter plot; to summarise the strength of a straight-line relationship, use the Pearson correlation r, which runs from −1 (perfect negative) through 0 (none) to +1 (perfect positive).

Scatter

  • RM: more rooms → higher value (r ≈ +0.70). Mostly linear.
  • LSTAT: higher share of lower-status residents → lower value (r ≈ −0.74). The cloud is curved — a straight line under-fits both ends. A scatter shows this; r alone wouldn't.
  • The red points along the top are the capped $50k homes — they visibly flatten the pattern.

Comparing groups

For a category vs. a number, compare the groups. Tracts on the Charles River ( of 506) average vs. elsewhere.

df.groupby("CHAS")["MEDV"].agg(["count", "mean", "median"])

Watch out for

Correlation ≠ causation

Adding rooms to a house doesn't automatically raise its value by r. Bigger homes cluster in wealthier areas.

Redundant features

RAD and TAX correlate at r = . They carry almost the same information (multicollinearity).

Non-linear patterns

r only measures straight lines. Always look at the scatter.

Lesson 6

Your EDA workflow

Here's the whole process in one script you can run on housing.csv (needs pandas, matplotlib, seaborn):

import pandas as pd
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt
import seaborn as sns

cols = ["CRIM","ZN","INDUS","CHAS","NOX","RM","AGE",
        "DIS","RAD","TAX","PTRATIO","B","LSTAT","MEDV"]
df = pd.read_csv("Data/housing.csv", sep=r"\s+", header=None, names=cols)

# 1. What do I have?
print(df.shape); print(df.dtypes); print(df.head())

# 2. Can I trust it?
print(df.isna().sum()); print(df.duplicated().sum())
print(df.describe().T)
print((df["MEDV"] == 50).sum(), "capped targets")

# 3. One variable at a time
df.hist(bins=30, figsize=(14, 10)); plt.tight_layout(); plt.show()
sns.boxplot(x=df["MEDV"]); plt.show()

# 4. Relationships
print(df.corr()["MEDV"].sort_values())
sns.heatmap(df.corr(), cmap="RdBu_r", center=0, annot=True, fmt=".2f")
plt.show()
sns.scatterplot(data=df, x="LSTAT", y="MEDV"); plt.show()
print(df.groupby("CHAS")["MEDV"].mean())

1. What is the main goal of EDA?

EDA is about understanding and generating questions. Confirming hypotheses and building models come afterwards.

2. CRIM has mean 3.61 and median 0.26. What does that tell you?

When the mean is much larger than the median, a long right tail of big values is inflating the mean.

3. 16 tracts have MEDV exactly 50.0, the maximum. Best first step?

A pile-up at a round maximum usually means censoring. Decide deliberately how to handle it (flag, drop, or use a method that handles censored data) — and document why.

4. LSTAT vs. MEDV has r = −0.74. Which statement is correct?

Correlation describes how variables move together, not cause and effect. And the scatter shows the relationship is actually curved.

5. RAD and TAX correlate at 0.91. Why does that matter?

Highly correlated features are redundant (multicollinearity). Linear models' coefficients become unstable; you might keep one or combine them.
